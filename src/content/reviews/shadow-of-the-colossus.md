## Boss Fights as Puzzles

Back in the 90s and 00s, many of us that grew up on gaming were familiar with the concept of a boss rush (I myself was introduced to it through The Arena in hit SNES video game, Kirby Super Star). My perception of Shadow of the Colossus was that Team ICO wanted to take this idea and flip it on its head; everything is slowed way down, from the pace of exploration to the nature of the fights. The systems in Shadow often ride the line between pressuring you with time limits while still requiring you to stop and consider.

The colossus fights in Shadow can be reasonably described as ponderous, in the literal sense that they often invite you to ponder how the creature is behaving. The first few mostly exist to familiarize you with the basics of your equipment, specifically choosing a biped first and quadruped second to get a feel for some different shapes. But as you get into the third and fourth fight, you realize it's way less about climbing and stabbing ASAP, and way more about "how the hell do I get up there?". Convincing a guy with a giant sword to smash the ground so you can climb up it was not invented by this game, but it's such an obvious 3 after 1 and 2! Then 4 (a common wall for players) really makes your life difficult by basically asking you to figure out all of the things a weird horse is going to do. It slowly meandering over to the holes that you hide in, getting frustrated, and stamping on them, was my "in" moment. I think a lot of people find it an "out" moment because figuring it out unguided legitimately involves trial and error, but not so much in the modern way that you might consider figuring out the attack tells in a FromSoft game. Sometimes the weird monster doesn't do what you expect, and you have to sort of be ok with that.

And then the actual plot twist of colossus 5, which involves tricking a bird to dive bomb you so you can grab onto its wings and stab it mid flight! And the secret here, one of the real geniuses of this game that I love, is that, mechanically, it's kind of still the same vibe as colossus 1. The music change (the music changing as you "solve" the puzzle is INSANELY satisfying, both here and in other games), the setting change as you ride into the air, is all set dressing on the same mechanics, but with enough presentation change that it can _feel_ different. Feeling different while staying in-style is a game design tactic that really hits, for me.

I could do a long run through of every colossus, and maybe I should at some point, but for now I just want to do a few quick hits of some of my favourite changeups and encounter designs:

- The 10th colossus, a sand serpent, whose fight asks you to engage with using your bow on horseback. I know a lot of people have some trouble figuring out how to do this one, but for me the fight is actually kind of a marvel of tuning, especially with respect to how much time you actually get to figure out and ine up backwards horseback arrow shots. Maybe I was just in the right age range, but the fear as I stared back at a giant snake following me like a shark lodged itself in my brain for years, and yet I was only ever actually hit by it a few times. The feel was right there!
- The 11th colossus, one of my favourite surprises, and I feel like if he came out today there would be such a push for him: he's just a little guy!! The "I've spent 10 fights climbing up and onto giant monsters, and now this one is barely bigger than me" is such a great little twist! I wish the actual encounter folded in known mechanics a little better, because it felt like a lot of custom work went into the torch interaction, which goes against one of the design pieces I love about the game. I think the way the 14th colossus, also a little guy, involves climbing and inducing attacks, fits in way better with the rest of the game. But a part of me really respects holding out on the little guys until well after the halfway point of the game, so it can be a real surprise.
- The 16th colossus, which, as a final encounter, obviously intends to switch things up. This is another one where I don't necessarily love the entire encounter, in particular because of how quickly you die doing the trial-and-error bit of the climbing section. But I think the changeup of giving you a colossus that switches up movement for a fucking _mega cannon_ as the final encounter is super sick, and navigating his upper extremities like a quick-time event actually kind of works for me, especially with getting paid off for spending the time to collect lizard tails during the game.


## Minimalized Controls

One thing I took away from this game, in terms of my own professional development, is squeezing out every drop from a minimalistic control scheme. At the end of the day, Shadow of the Colossus has about 5 buttons: sword, jump, climb, bow, and whistle. And then surprise, there's actually a bunch of moves you can do with these together! Did you know that if you jump and then press the right buttons, you do a jump stab instead of a jump slash? This counts as a charged up stab attack! There's a couple of colossi that you can lure by waving your body out in the open air, but a number of them can actually be lured or distracted by doing your whistle, which is normally just to summon your horse! The game eventually makes you learn that you can turn around on your horse, and fire your bow backwards, but you can actually stand up as you ride, and jump off, and even grab onto birds! That's kinda crazy. Nobody expects you to do that. There's even a secret garden right on top of the starting area! How did you even find that?? Wild.

As much as I'm a lover of adventure games like Zelda and Psychonauts, which give you a lot of tools with more-or-less prescribed operation points, Shadow of the Colossus made me really fall in love with the idea of a minimalist control scheme with a bunch of applications that you discover as you go. I think the "discover as you go" aspect, especially, has come through strongly in the indie space. I'm mostly thinking of more pared-down Zelda-likes, like Tunic or Master Key, which will tell you things, but will also let you discover, or fail to discover, a lot of things on your own.


## Pacing

I love to juxtapose the bosses in this game to those designed by studios like Platinum, which often gets a lot of value out of bombast and high pace. Shadow of the Colossus puts a lot of effort into slowing things down. One fair criticism is that some colossi take a long time to find, and the world design can explicitly make your life difficult in that respect. But it's a key part of giving you downtime, whether you want to turn the device off or not. Reflecting on fights after doing them, reflecting on the weird black tentacles that stabbed you in the ass and whether you could have gotten away (maybe a future arena will let you?), helps it stick, I think. Before the fights, during the fights, and even the brief window after the fight, the rising action is long and slow, and the falling action is sudden in a way that invites you to relax, because it's also inevitable. 

The inevitability of the story is also an aspect of this point, where a lot isn't explained to you straight up, so you sort of slowly internalize that maybe you're not doing a good thing here. A lot of indie titles take this to more extended, meta-game places (I'm thinking of Toby Fox's output, Undertale's genocide route in particular, or similar titles like OneShot). But I think there's something to be said - maybe just an aspect of the era that still resonates with me - about letting a story, where you play as a character ostensibly making a mistake, just exist as it is. You can see it coming, you can stop playing if you want, and how you feel about the reality of the situation is up to you.


## Game Over

Anyway, I love this game for being the opposite of high octane action, and hopefully that comes through in my writing. One million stars out of 5.
